    About

    Based on “Which European language am I reading?” by Øystein H. Brekke (@oysteib) and Max Fras (@maxfras), 2021. Interactive version by Daniel Pradilla.

    Brekke made the original flowchart and extended it to other alphabets. Fras had a designer redraw it as this metro map (the designer is not named in the posts). The transcription uses the 4096×2896 original attached to Fras's post.

    How it works

    Each circle asks whether the text contains certain letters. A light-blue line marked Y leaves for yes, a salmon line marked N for no. Every route ends at a flag with the language's own name under it. The start station f g r sends texts with any of those Latin letters into the Latin network and everything else into the Greek, Armenian, Georgian, Hebrew and Cyrillic network. The chart groups languages by the letters they use, not by family, and the flags mark countries, regions or peoples, not languages.

    Strict rules

    • A station says yes when the text contains at least one of its letters. Upper and lower case count the same.
    • Letters match whole: a decomposed a + U+0308 counts as ä, but r does not match the base of a decomposed ř.
    • Only three variants are folded: c'h with c’h or cʼh; l·l with l‧l or ŀl; the palochka ӏ with Ӏ.
    • Nothing else is folded. Turkish ş is not Romanian ș. Latin ə is not Cyrillic ә. Latin i, j are not Cyrillic і, ј. Many Caucasian texts type the palochka as Latin I, l or digit 1; those do not match. Station panels in Maze show the code points.
    • Text with no Latin, Greek, Cyrillic, Armenian, Georgian or Hebrew letter still gets a route, which ends at Bulgarian, with a notice.

    Quirks of the chart

    The chart assumes a long, representative sample with every letter of the language and no loanwords. Short or unusual texts take odd routes, and the app shows them as they are.
