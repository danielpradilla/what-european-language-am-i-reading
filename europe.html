    About the Europe map

    Based on “Which European language am I reading?” by Øystein H. Brekke (@oysteib) and Max Fras (@maxfras), 2021. Interactive version by Daniel Pradilla.

    What this map shows

    The poster sorts the 106 languages by the letters they use. This page draws the same 106 languages as a metro map shaped roughly like Europe. Each station is a language with its flag and its own name, and each line is a language family: Indo-European languages get one line per branch (Romance, Germanic, Slavic…), the other families get one line each. Branches of a line fork like metro lines.

    Dashed walkways join neighbouring stations of different lines where people live side by side, such as Basque with Spanish and French or Maltese with Italian. They show language contact and say nothing about kinship. Esperanto has no homeland, so it sits in a dashed ring in the middle. Romani is spoken across Europe: its two stations sit in central Europe and in the Balkans, on the Indo-Iranian line that continues to Ossetian in the Caucasus.

    Where the letter chart and the family map disagree is the interesting part. Finnish, Estonian and Hungarian are relatives, yet the poster puts them far apart because of their letters. Detect replays the poster's route on this map, one letter question at a time, so you can watch the letters cut across families and geography.

    Modes

    • Explore: tap a station for its language card, or hover a line for a short introduction to its family. Arrow keys hop between neighbouring stations.
    • Detect: paste, type or fetch a text. At every letter station of the poster, the languages ruled out fade from the map, with a counter, until one station lights up. The strict matching rules are the same as on the poster.
    • Guess: ten rounds. Read a sample text and tap the station where it is spoken: 100 points for the exact language, 40 for a language on the same line (“Right family”). Each answer shows the poster's route for that text. Scores are kept on this device.

    How the map was made

    Station positions start from each language's coordinates in Glottolog (Hammarström, Forkel, Haspelmath & Bank, CC BY 4.0), projected onto a plane and then schematised by hand: lines run horizontally, vertically or at 45°, and stations sit on an even grid. The geography is approximate on purpose. Crowded areas get more room than their real size, so the Caucasus (22 stations) and Sápmi (7) are drawn enlarged, like a city centre on a metro map. The families follow Glottolog's classification, simplified for the map. Line colours are scenery and carry no state meaning.

    The faint coastline behind the lines is Natural Earth (public domain), simplified and bent so that the seas fall between the schematic stations. Flags come from Wikimedia Commons and other sources; they mark places and peoples, not languages. Sources and licences are in CREDITS.md. Pasted and typed text stays in your browser. Only a URL you ask to fetch goes to this site's api/fetch.php.
